5 Critical Clauses Every Founder Needs in Their Co-Founder Agreement

Aditi Rao• October 15, 2025• 6 min read

Introduction

The honeymoon phase of a startup is exciting. Ideas are flowing, caffeine is abundant, and you and your co-founder are ready to conquer the world. But what happens when things get tough? Or when one person wants to leave? Without a written agreement, these scenarios can kill a company before it even takes off.

A Co-Founder Agreement isn't just a legal formality; it's a roadmap for your business relationship. It sets expectations, roles, and most importantly, exit strategies. Here are the five critical clauses you cannot afford to skip.

1. Equity Ownership and Vesting Schedules

Splitting equity 50-50 might seem fair now, but is it functional? You need to decide who gets what based on contribution, capital, and risk. More importantly, vesting is crucial.

Standard Vesting: A typical clause involves a 4-year vesting schedule with a 1-year cliff. This means if a co-founder leaves within the first year, they walk away with nothing. This aligns incentives and protects the company's cap table.

2. Roles and Responsibilities

Ambiguity breeds conflict. Who makes the final call on product? Who handles fundraising? Clearly defining roles (e.g., CEO vs. CTO) helps in day-to-day decision making and prevents stepping on toes.

  • Decision Making: Define which decisions require a majority vote and which require unanimity (e.g., selling the company, taking on debt).

3. Intellectual Property (IP) Assignment

This is a deal-breaker for investors. All IP created by the founders for the company must belong to the company, not the individuals. If a co-founder leaves, they shouldn't take the code or the brand with them.

4. Restriction on Transfer of Shares (ROFR)

You don't want to wake up one day and find out your co-founder sold their stake to a stranger (or worse, a competitor). A Right of First Refusal (ROFR) clause ensures that if a founder wants to sell their shares, they must first offer them to the company or the other founders.

5. Exit and Dissolution Strategy

It's uncomfortable to talk about the end at the beginning, but it's necessary. What happens if the startup fails? How are assets distributed? What if a founder is fired for cause? Having these protocols in writing saves expensive legal battles down the road.

Conclusion
